## Candidate 1 — Independent replication of failure-transparent-agents (reproducibility)

The repo (https://github.com/junru-zhu/failure-transparent-agents, MIT, public, created 2026-09-10) benchmarks whether LLM agents admit tool failure instead of hallucinating success: 100 frozen tasks, deterministic failed-tool simulator, 1,800 confirmatory responses across three models, model-judged. The published confirmatory claim: false-success falls from 32.0% (baseline) to 11.8% (transparency instruction) to 1.3% (evidence contract). The README's "Extend the benchmark" section lists "independent replications on new models and serving stacks" and "judge-robustness and cross-judge agreement analysis" as high-impact contributions, and ships a reproduction guide, research protocol, frozen model-judge report (SHA-256-published labels), and completion audit. Offline validation (`make check-dataset`, `make test`, `make full-scale-validation`) makes zero network calls and uses only the Python standard library; live collection is opt-in and budget-capped (the Claude arm cost $2.38 for 600 responses). Distinct agent contributions: dataset-integrity verification against published hashes, offline re-analysis re-deriving every table, fresh-sample collection with a different judge in the participant's own environment, human annotation of a judge-label sample (falsification), provenance review (paper claims vs. frozen artifacts), report writing. A failed week still produces a useful public finding: either the numbers re-derive or they don't. Caveats: the author himself states v0.3.0 is model-judge-only and not human-validated; the replication must carry that limitation plainly, and it targets the confirmatory three-model result, not the post-confirmatory six-model extension.

## Candidate 2 — Data.gov link-health audit, one agency slice (public records)

A Congressional Research Service report covered by MuckRock on 2026-08-19 (https://www.muckrock.com/news/archives/2026/aug/19/crs-report-on-datagov-reveals-major-flaws-in-the-federal-dataset-portal/) found Data.gov is a metadata directory, not a vault: when an agency reorganizes its site, catalog links break or datasets vanish silently at the next harvest, with no central backup. Data.gov's own user guide (https://github.com/gsa/datagov-11ty/blob/HEAD/pages/user-guide.md) states it does not fix broken links directly and routes reporters to agency contacts. Roughly 3,000 datasets were removed across 2025 (count contested). The bounded question: for one agency's catalog slice (e.g., CDC; slice fixed at freeze from the agency's public data.json), which accessURLs are dead, redirected, or erroring? Federal datasets are U.S. Government Works (public domain). Contributions: slice definition and harvest, liveness checking in participants' own environments, manual verification of ambiguous cases, stale-metadata review, report writing. Evaluation is deterministic: each claim is a (dataset, URL, status, timestamp) assertion the evaluator re-checks by hand on a random sample. Intrinsic value: there is no public, current, systematic link-health dataset for the catalog, and the CRS report shows the failure mode is structural, not anecdotal.

## Candidate 3 — Human-confirmed WCAG audit of high-traffic federal dataset pages (accessibility)

The WebAIM Million February 2026 report (via https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/web-accessibility-statistics-2026-wcag-lawsuit-data and https://wcagc.com/blog/webaim-million-2026) found 95.9% of the top million home pages had detectable WCAG 2 failures, up from 94.8% — the first regression in six years — averaging 56.1 errors per page. The six most common classes: low-contrast text (83.9%), missing alt text (53.1%), unlabeled inputs (51%), empty links (46.3%), empty buttons (30.6%), missing document language (13.5%). WebAIM covers home pages only, while AI search now routes users to internal pages (https://news.designrush.com/ai-search-website-accessibility-fixes). The bounded question: on a frozen list of the most-visited federal dataset pages, which of the six error classes are actually present, each confirmed by a human reviewer? Contributions: page-list curation, automated pre-scan in own environments, human confirmation/falsification of each flag, alt-text quality scoring against a rubric, plain-language report. Evaluation: every finding carries a human confirmation note; the evaluator spot-checks against live pages. Intrinsic value: a human-confirmed dataset on the deep pages people actually land on — the layer WebAIM's census does not cover. Honest caveat: no agency asked for this; it must be framed as research, not a shaming exercise.

## Candidate 4 — FOIA status ledger for the Stanford AI-chatbot request campaign (public records)

In April 2026 a team led by Stanford's Alex Spangher filed 24 federal FOIA plus 81 state/local records requests seeking government AI-chatbot interaction logs, policies, and retention schedules (https://coloradofoic.org/are-the-governments-conversations-with-ai-accessible-under-public-records-laws-and-foia/). Preliminary findings: deployments cataloged at 53 federal agencies, all 50 states, 77 local governments; first corpus of 3,216 government AI chat threads; top barriers are "no records responsive" claims and extreme fees (Colorado: $9M). The requests live as public MuckRock pages; e.g., the EOUSA request (https://www.muckrock.com/foi/united-states-of-america-10/ai-chatbot-interaction-logs-executive-office-for-united-states-attorneys-209329/, filed April 7, 2026, case EOUSA-2026-003224) shows 4 communications, 0 files, acknowledged-but-unfulfilled — verified today. The bounded question: for each of the 105 requests, what is the current disposition (released / denied / fee-assessed / no-records / pending), with citation and date? Contributions: request inventory, per-request status extraction, double-review of ambiguous dispositions, fee/no-records pattern analysis, report. Evaluation: every ledger row cites its MuckRock page; the evaluator spot-checks. This is pure review work — no code involved at all. Intrinsic value: the first structured public ledger of an unprecedented transparency campaign, documenting the "no records" and fee barriers in citable form.

## Candidate 5 — DCAT-US catalog link-liveness checker (open-source research tool)

GSA's own docs describe a manual broken-link reporting flow (https://github.com/gsa/datagov-11ty/blob/HEAD/pages/contact.md), and their automated weekly link QA covers only their documentation site (https://github.com/GSA/resources.data.gov/blob/HEAD/README.md, "Link Checker Report" issue #811) — not the 364,095-dataset catalog. The bounded question: can a team in one week ship a reviewed, documented, fixture-tested checker that takes an agency data.json and emits a signed machine-readable liveness report? New code under MIT; fixture catalog with known-dead/known-live URLs as the deterministic test. Evaluation: the reviewer runs the fixture in their own approved environment plus code review — no arbitrary execution. Contributions: spec, implementation, fixture design, docs, fixture provenance review. Intrinsic value: reusable by any open-data publisher. Honest caveat: this is the most speculative candidate — no maintainer asked for it by name, and a tool without an adopter is a demo, not infrastructure.

## Recommendation: Candidate 1

Run the independent replication of failure-transparent-agents.

Why collaboration helps here: decomposability only. The work splits into
genuinely distinct, combinable contributions that different agents can own
in parallel — one verifies dataset hashes against the published SHA-256s,
one re-runs the offline analysis and re-derives every table, one collects
a small fresh sample with a different judge in its own environment, one
hand-annotates a sample of judge labels to falsify them, one does
provenance review (do the paper's claims match the frozen artifacts?), one
writes the report. No contribution duplicates another, and they combine
into a single verdict. This is a claim about the shape of the work, not
about speed or throughput: no matched comparison of collaborative vs.
solo performance was run.

Why it fits the constraints: Tier 1 needs no network, no API keys, no spend — Python standard library only. Nothing submitted ever executes on our machines; participants run the public repo in their own environments and submit findings as data. Evaluation is deterministic against a frozen gold standard.

What a failed week still produces: "the published numbers did (or did not) re-derive from the frozen labels" is a useful public result either way. A non-replication is publishable, checkable, and honestly caveated — exactly the kind of negative result worth preserving.

Why it wins over the others: it is the only candidate where the target work is explicitly invited in writing by the party who would benefit (the README lists independent replication as a high-impact contribution). Candidates 2–4 have plausible beneficiaries but no invitation; candidate 5 has neither invitation nor adopter.

## Gaps before anyone promises a public event

- Isolation: the submission format (findings as data, never code) must be specified in the invitation; there is currently no intake mechanism.

- Evaluator: no named reviewer has agreed to anything. Options: publish the deterministic acceptance procedure in advance (Tier 1 re-derivation must match within the paper's stated tolerances) and name who runs it, or recruit a named third-party reviewer. Do not promise "review by the maintainer" — he has not agreed.
- Scope lock: the replication targets the confirmatory three-model result only, not the post-confirmatory six-model extension; and the model-judge-only limitation must appear in the invitation, not be discovered later.
